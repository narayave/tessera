# Concept

What Tessera is, who it's for, and what it refuses to be.

---

## The problem

A group of people are in a room. Some know each other; most don't. Left alone,
they will spend the evening in the clusters they arrived in and leave having met
nobody new.

This is a solved problem in theory — put strangers on a shared task and they bond
— and an unsolved one in practice, because most mechanisms for doing it are worse
than doing nothing. Icebreakers are hated for good reasons. Party games at this
scale mostly collapse into a few confident people performing while everyone else
waits their turn.

Tessera is an attempt at a mechanism without those failure modes.

## Who it's for

Roughly **15–40 people**, in one venue, who need to stop being strangers fast.

The reference case is a house party, which is the hardest version: multiple
rooms, uneven acoustics, one home router, drinks in hand, guests arriving across
a forty-minute window, and no sober referee available. If it works there, it
works at an offsite.

**Runtime is 45–50 minutes.** It's an opener, not an evening. The game ends and
the party starts. It's the excuse for people to talk, and then it gets out of the
way.

## Goals, in priority order

1. **People genuinely meet each other.** Not "were in the same room as." Talked
   to, did something with, would recognise later.
2. **Everyone has fun.** Low stakes throughout. Nobody feels stupid, exposed,
   singled out, or left standing at the edge.
3. **A quiet homage to the guest of honour** — ambient, never interrogative. The
   picture at the end is the whole of it.
4. **A real open-source project.** Someone else should be able to point it at
   their own event, supply three pieces of data, and have it work.

## Non-goals

- **Testing anyone's knowledge of the guest of honour.** Half the room may barely
  know them. Any design where knowing them better means winning more is
  backwards.
- **Being an impressive puzzle.** Difficulty is not the point and actively hurts:
  every unit of intellectual difficulty converts a social blocker into a private
  one.
- **Filling the whole party.** A 45-minute opener that knows when to stop.
- **Producing a winner.** There is no opposing side.

---

## The three commitments

Everything else is negotiable. These are not.

### One cohesive game

Not a sequence of mini-games. Not modes. Not a portfolio of activities with a
theme stretched over them. Engagement dies when the experience fragments — people
stop understanding what they're doing and start waiting for it to be over.

One board, one loop, the entire session. Escalation changes a parameter of that
loop; it never switches to a different activity.

### Nothing is solvable alone, and the blocker is always social

What stands between you and progress is never a puzzle you can't crack. It's a
person you haven't met. Every time.

This is what makes it an icebreaker rather than a quiz with a social veneer, and
it's why difficulty is actively harmful — a hard puzzle lets a clever person
solve it privately and skip the part that matters.

### Cooperative, with no opposition at all

No other team. Nobody eliminated, nothing contested, no territory taken from
anyone, no leaderboard.

The only available action is to find more people and join with them. The
structure is convergent by construction: it begins as one person per piece and
ends with the whole room connected, because that is the only state in which the
picture exists.

This was arrived at the long way round, through several competitive designs —
land grabs, duels, territory contests. Every one hit the same wall: any mechanic
that produces losers produces guests standing at the edge of the room with
nothing to do, and those are exactly the people the event exists to include.

---

## How the design got here

Four shapes, in order:

1. **A pooled puzzle hunt** — solve mostly alone, combine at the end. Rejected:
   the social mechanic was bolted on rather than being the substrate.
2. **A territory land grab** — claim tiles, contest them, absorb losing teams.
   Rejected: competition among strangers produces disengaged losers.
3. **Crew rounds** — small teams, short high-intensity rounds, merging between
   them. Mechanically strong; rejected for cohesion, because it makes the night a
   sequence of separate small games.
4. **The mosaic** — one board, one loop, purely cooperative, adjacency-driven.

The through-line that survived all four: **the room is the puzzle.** Content
comes from what guests submit about themselves before the event, never from
hand-authoring. That's what makes the finding social, and it's what makes this a
reusable project rather than a one-off.
